\section{ATLAS Open-Data Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:benchmark}

We use \(Z\rightarrow ee\) events from the \texttt{Sh\_2211\_Zee\_maxHTpTV2} Monte Carlo sample (dataset ID 700320) in \texttt{DAOD\_PHYSLITE} format, streamed directly from public-access ATLAS Open-Data. The full sample combines 20 \texttt{DAOD\_PHYSLITE} files drawn from two production tags (37621257, 37621269), producing 1,524,196 di-electron events after selection, of which 1,509,910 (99.1\%) are opposite-charge and 14,286 (0.9\%) are same-charge.

To assign a charge misidentification level for each reconstructed electron, we need to know whether its assigned reco-charge matches its true generator-level charge. Every reconstructed electron carries an \texttt{ElementLink} (\texttt{TruthLink}) to the corresponding truth particle, with an associated truth-level charge, $p_T$, and $\eta$.

A QmisID label is then assinged independent of any detector-level identification algorithm.
\[
\ell^{\mathrm{truth}} = \mathbb{1}\!\left[q^{\mathrm{reco}} \neq q^{\mathrm{truth}}\right],
\]

Events are selected by requiring exactly two reconstructed electrons, each with \(p_T > 20\)~GeV, both truth matched.

\section{Testing the Classifier: Synthetic di-Electron Benchmark}

First, we test the di-electron classifier in a controlled setting. A flexible discrete-kernel model (a \texttt{FiniteKernel}) is trained on the truth-matched electrons, giving a reference flip probability \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_T)\) conditioned on single electron truth kinematics $p_t^T$, $eta^T$. Treating \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)\) as ground truth, the synthetic SC/OC samples are generated by stochastically flipping the reconstructed charge of each electron in the di-electron dataset with probability \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)\). in this benchmark the true flip function is known exactly.

The di-electron classifier of \cref{sec:method} is trained on this synthetic benchmark dataset, and we test to see how well the classifier learns \(p(SC|x_true)\). 

The classifier performs well on the synthetic benchmark, by far outperforming the state-of-the-art likelihood calculation. Performance, althought still good, is slightly degraded on real open-data. Here we outline resolution effects of the detector, where continuous detector response functions come into play but go beyond the scope of this paper. A method to simultaneously learn the continuous component of this 'smear' together with the discrete probability of charge misidentification is currently under investigation.
